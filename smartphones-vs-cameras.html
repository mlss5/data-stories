The Camera in Everyone’s Pocket

Smartphone shipments vs. the collapse of the compact camera, 2007–2025

Data Story
Consumer Electronics
Smartphones
Cameras
Published

September 5, 2026

Data Story  ·  IDC  ·  CIPA

BlackBerry shipped a camera phone before Apple shipped a phone at all. The BlackBerry Pearl arrived in September 2006 with a 1.3-megapixel camera — RIM had deliberately left cameras off every earlier model, because its enterprise customers didn’t want employees photographing sensitive offices. The iPhone launched nine months later. What followed wasn’t just BlackBerry’s collapse; it was the compact camera’s. By the time smartphone shipments passed a billion units a year, the point-and-shoot camera — the device the camera phone was actually competing with — had been reduced to a rounding error.

Two industries, one dataset problem

The camera side of this story is CIPA’s — the same worldwide shipment data used in our earlier post on the DSLR-to-mirrorless transition. The smartphone side comes from IDC’s Worldwide Quarterly Mobile Phone Tracker, the industry-standard source that most outlets (and Statista’s own smartphone-shipment statistics) ultimately cite. The two trackers don’t share a methodology — IDC counts all Android and iOS manufacturers combined, CIPA counts a defined list of Japanese-headquartered camera brands — but both report worldwide unit shipments to the same rough standard, which is what makes overlaying them meaningful.

2007–2009 smartphone totals are reconstructed estimates (IDC’s own press archive gets sparse before its 2010–2011 releases), interpolated between known points: Apple’s own reported 3.3 million iPhones in 2007, RIM’s roughly 20% share of a 175-million-unit market in 2009, and Nokia’s continued Symbian dominance through the period. 2010 onward are IDC’s own published totals, cited repeatedly across its press releases. Compact camera figures are CIPA’s, identical to the previous post.

The pioneer that got left behind

BlackBerry’s own trajectory is a case study within the case study. RIM held its cameras back for years on principle — the BlackBerry 7230 (2003) had Bluetooth and a color screen but no camera; corporate IT departments and government buildings were actively hostile to camera phones at the time, and RIM’s whole business was selling into those buildings. The Pearl broke with that policy in 2006, and it worked: RIM’s shipments roughly tripled over the next four years, peaking around 2011. Then BlackBerry OS’s browser, app ecosystem, and touchscreen kept losing to iOS and Android, the camera-first feature that once differentiated the Pearl became table stakes on every phone, and BlackBerry’s hardware shipments collapsed even faster than they’d grown.

BlackBerry (RIM) worldwide smartphone shipments, calendar-year estimates reconstructed from RIM/BlackBerry quarterly and fiscal-year filings (SEC Forms 6-K and 40-F) and contemporaneous market-share reporting; RIM’s fiscal year ran March–February, so calendar-year figures are approximate. The feature that won wasn’t the camera

It’s tempting to read this as “camera phones killed cameras,” and directionally that’s right — but BlackBerry’s own arc shows the camera alone was never the differentiator. RIM added a perfectly serviceable camera in 2006 and gained share for years afterward; it lost that share to companies that paired a camera with instant sharing, an app ecosystem, and a touchscreen built around photos and video rather than email. The compact camera didn’t lose to “a camera in a phone” — it lost to a phone where the camera was one part of a always-with-you, always-connected package that no dedicated camera, however good the sensor, could match. Which is also, per the previous post, exactly the gap mirrorless cameras and revived point-and-shoots are now trying to compete on different terms altogether: image quality and a deliberate, single-purpose object, rather than convenience.
