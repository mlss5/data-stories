Life Expectancy and National Income: A Cross-Sectional and Time-Series Correlation Analysis

Reported following the STROBE framework for observational studies

Machine Learning for Social Science  ·  Boston, MA

This post treats a familiar relationship — life expectancy and income — as a small observational study, reported in the structure epidemiologists use for cross-sectional and cohort research (the STROBE guidelines). The aim is not the epidemiology; it is to use a rigorous reporting frame to ask a question this whole series has been circling: does a correlation observed across units at one point in time describe the same relationship as a correlation observed within one unit over time?

Abstract

Design. Observational, correlational study combining a cross-sectional comparison across countries with a longitudinal time series within a single country. Objective. To estimate the Pearson correlation between life expectancy and GDP per capita (i) across 175 countries at one point in time (2023–2024) and (ii) within the United States annually from 1960 to 2024, and to compare the two. Methods. Publicly available national statistics; Pearson correlation coefficients computed on both untransformed and log-transformed income. Results. Both designs show a strong positive association: r = 0.86 (log income) in the cross-section (n = 175, p < 10−50) and r = 0.97 (log income) in the US time series (n = 65, p < 10−39). Conclusion. The correlation is strong and positive under both designs, but a correlation coefficient alone cannot distinguish a direct effect of income from a shared trend or common cause — a limitation addressed further in the discussion.

Introduction

Background and rationale

The association between national income and population health is one of the best-documented empirical regularities in economic demography, generally credited to Samuel Preston’s 1975 cross-national comparison of life expectancy and GDP per capita (the “Preston curve”). The relationship is concave: a given increase in income buys much more life expectancy in a poor country than in a rich one. It is also, definitionally, a cross-sectional finding — different countries compared at a single point in time.

A separate and logically independent question is whether the same association holds within a country as it develops over time. This distinction matters because a relationship that is real and strong in a cross-section need not describe what happens to any single unit as it moves through time — a problem long recognized in the social sciences as the ecological fallacy (Robinson, 1950) and, in this specific empirical setting, documented directly by Preston himself: national time series of income growth explain only a modest share of a country’s own gains in life expectancy over the twentieth century, with the cross-sectional curve itself shifting upward decade by decade. This series has now checked that same cross-section/time-series distinction for three different pairs of variables (women’s labor force participation and income; urbanization and income; and here, life expectancy and income), because it is a general methodological question, not one specific to any variable pair.

Objectives

  1. To estimate the Pearson correlation between life expectancy and GDP per capita across countries at one point in time (a cross-sectional design).
  2. To estimate the Pearson correlation between the same two variables within a single country (the United States) across a 65-year period (a time-series design).
  3. To compare the two estimates and consider what a simple correlation coefficient can, and cannot, establish about the relationship in each design.

Methods

Study design

Two designs are reported side by side: (i) a cross-sectional design, in which each country contributes a single observation at approximately the same point in time; and (ii) a time-series design, in which a single country (the United States) contributes one observation per year over 65 consecutive years. No intervention, treatment assignment, or manipulation of any kind was involved in either design; both are purely observational.

Setting

The cross-sectional design covers all countries for which both variables were available in the sources described below, observed in the most recent year each source publishes (GDP per capita: 2023; life expectancy: most recent CIA World Factbook estimate, referenced 2024). The time-series design covers the United States, annually, from 1960 (the first year both source series report) to 2024.

Participants (units of analysis)

Cross-section: All sovereign states and territories for which a numeric value was available for both life expectancy and GDP per capita in the sources below, matched by country name (with manual reconciliation of naming differences, e.g., “South Korea” vs. “Korea, Rep.”). Of 229 countries and territories with a life expectancy figure, 175 (76%) had a matching GDP per capita figure and were included; the remainder lacked a GDP estimate in the source used (chiefly small territories and countries without recent World Bank national accounts data, e.g., North Korea, Cuba, Eritrea) and were excluded.

Time series: All calendar years 1960–2024 for the United States for which both series report a value. No years were missing; n = 65.

Variables

Outcome (both designs): Life expectancy at birth, in years — the average number of years a newborn would live if age-specific mortality rates at the time of observation stayed constant over its lifetime.

Exposure (both designs): GDP per capita — gross domestic product divided by population. Cross-sectional GDP per capita is expressed in current US dollars; time-series GDP per capita is expressed in constant 2010 US dollars (to remove the effect of US inflation over the 65-year window, which a current-dollar figure would otherwise confound with real income growth).

Bias

Three sources of potential bias are noted. First, the cross-section combines life expectancy from one producer (CIA World Factbook) and GDP per capita from another (World Bank), reported in different years (~2024 and 2023 respectively) and compiled by different aggregator sites; this is a comparability concern rather than a random-error concern, since it could shift the estimated correlation in either direction depending on how each source’s methodology and reference year diverge. Second, both cross-sectional variables, and both time-series variables, come from a single World Bank lineage in the time-series case, reducing (but not eliminating) that specific concern for the within-country design. Third, and most importantly for interpretation: no adjustment was made, in either design, for variables that plausibly affect both life expectancy and income at once (education, disease environment, war, and — specific to the time-series design — the passage of calendar time itself, during which medical technology improved independently of any single country’s income growth). This is addressed further under Limitations.

Study size

Study size was determined by data availability rather than by a power calculation: all countries with matched data in the cross-section (n = 175), and all years with matched data in the US time series (n = 65).

Quantitative variables

GDP per capita was analyzed in two forms in both designs: untransformed (dollars) and natural-log-transformed. The log transform was included because the Preston curve is a well-documented concave relationship — equal dollar increases in income are associated with larger life-expectancy gains at low income than at high income — and a linear (Pearson) correlation on untransformed income will understate the strength of a genuinely monotonic but curved association. Life expectancy was analyzed untransformed (years) in both designs.

Statistical methods

All associations are reported as the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, computed the same way in both designs — the only difference is what the index runs over:

Cross-section (country i = 1…n):

r = Σi(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / √[Σi(xi − x̄)² · Σi(yi − ȳ)²]

Time series (year t = 1…T, single country):

r = Σt(xt − x̄)(yt − ȳ) / √[Σt(xt − x̄)² · Σt(yt − ȳ)²]

No regression model was fit in either design; only the correlation coefficient, its two-sided p-value (against the null of r = 0), and sample size are reported. This choice is deliberate and is returned to in the Discussion.

Why a correlation coefficient is a valid tool here, and where it stops being one. A Pearson correlation is a valid summary of the strength and direction of a linear (or, after transformation, monotonic) association between two variables, and its significance test is valid whenever observations are independent and the usual regularity conditions hold. It becomes an unreliable guide to the underlying relationship in at least three situations relevant here. First, when the association is driven by a third variable that independently moves both x and y — classically, a confounder. Second, when the data are aggregated units rather than the individual-level units the relationship is really about (the ecological fallacy): a correlation between country-level averages can differ in sign or magnitude from the correlation that would be observed within any single country, and vice versa. Third, in time series specifically, when both variables share a common trend — two unrelated series that both rise steadily over decades will correlate strongly even absent any causal link between them, because “time” itself is the omitted third variable. All three concerns are directly relevant to interpreting the results below, and are addressed in the Discussion.

Why life expectancy and GDP per capita are a good pair for illustrating this. The relationship is real, large, well-replicated, and has a plausible direct mechanism (income buys nutrition, sanitation, and medical care) — so it is not a spurious or trivial example. But it is simultaneously a textbook case for every one of the three correlation pitfalls above: countries differ on many confounders correlated with income (education, governance, disease burden); the cross-sectional relationship is explicitly known, from Preston’s own work, to have shifted over historical time in a way that a single correlation coefficient cannot reveal; and the US time series in particular spans a period (1960–2024) of dramatic medical progress that plausibly drove much of the life-expectancy gain independent of income growth. It is, in other words, a relationship where “the correlation is strong” is true and also nowhere near the end of the story.

Results

175 of 229 countries and territories with a life expectancy estimate (76%) had a matching GDP per capita estimate and were included in the cross-section. All 65 years from 1960 to 2024 were included in the US time series (100% of the available window).

Discussion

Key results

Both the cross-sectional and the time-series design show a strong, positive, statistically significant correlation between life expectancy and GDP per capita, and in both designs the relationship strengthens under a log transform of income. Taken at face value, the two designs agree: countries with higher income tend to have higher life expectancy, and the United States’ own life expectancy has risen closely alongside its own income over 65 years.

Limitations

This is a correlational analysis of unadjusted, aggregated national statistics, and its limitations follow directly from the three concerns raised in Methods. No confounders were controlled for, so neither r cannot separate a direct income→health effect from confounding by education, governance, disease environment, or (in the time series) simple calendar time, during which antibiotics, vaccination, and neonatal care advanced independent of any single country’s income growth. The cross-section mixes two data producers observed in different years, a comparability limitation noted above. And a single Pearson r, by construction, reports only the strength of the association at the level analyzed — it says nothing about whether the same relationship (the same slope, the same curve) holds across the two designs, only that a relationship of some strength holds in each. A companion piece in this series (the “Preston curve” post on the Goldin data pattern) shows directly, by plotting the US time path against the cross-sectional curve, that the two need not coincide even when both correlations are strong.

Interpretation

A cautious reading of these results is that income and life expectancy move together, strongly, both across countries and within the United States over time — but a correlation coefficient alone cannot say whether income is doing the causal work, in either design, or how much of each estimate reflects a genuine income effect versus a shared trend or common cause. The very high time-series correlation (r = 0.97 on log income) is exactly the pattern one would also expect to see if life expectancy and income were both simply rising over the same 65 years for largely unrelated reasons (medical technology improving on its own trajectory; income growing on its own) — the correlation coefficient, by design, cannot distinguish these two stories from each other.

Generalizability

The cross-sectional estimate describes the relationship across the specific set of 175 countries included here at one point in time and should not be assumed to describe the relationship within any individual country as it develops; the time-series estimate describes the United States specifically and should not be assumed to generalize to other countries, which may follow a different path (as this series has already shown directly for female labor force participation, where three of four countries with long-run data showed no U-shape at all despite a robust cross-sectional U).

Conclusion and next steps

Both designs here return a strong, statistically significant, unadjusted correlation between income and life expectancy, and the log-income version is stronger than the linear one in each case — a first, purely descriptive answer to whether “the correlation holds” across designs. What an unadjusted correlation coefficient cannot deliver is a quantified relationship one could use to predict, compare magnitudes across the two designs on a common scale, or begin separating a shared time trend from a direct association. The next post in this series will fit an unadjusted ordinary least squares (OLS) regression to both the cross-sectional and time-series data, which will add: an explicit slope (years of life expectancy per log-dollar of income) directly comparable between the two designs; an R² describing how much of the variance in life expectancy each design’s income measure accounts for; and residual plots that can start to reveal where the two designs’ relationships diverge, rather than only whether a correlation exists in each. That step remains, deliberately, still unadjusted for confounders — a fully adjusted model is a further step beyond it.
