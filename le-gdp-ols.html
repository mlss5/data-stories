Does Income Growth Raise Life Expectancy? Country-by-Country Regressions in Levels and Five-Year Differences

An observational study reported following the STROBE guidelines

Machine Learning for Social Science  ·  Boston, MA

The previous post in this series found that life expectancy and GDP per capita are strongly correlated within most countries over time, but that the correlation largely disappears when you look at year-over-year changes. A correlation says how tightly two series move together; it does not say by how much. This post fits a simple ordinary least squares (OLS) regression for each country, first in levels and then in five-year differences, to put a number on the question: when a country’s income grows, how much does its life expectancy change?

Abstract

Design. Observational study using country-specific time-series regressions. Objective. To estimate, for each country, the association between GDP per capita and life expectancy in levels, and between five-year changes in GDP per capita and five-year changes in life expectancy. Setting and participants. 106 countries with World Bank data on both variables from 1960, observed annually from 1960 to 2024 (levels) and over twelve non-overlapping five-year intervals from 1960 to 2020 (differences). Methods. Unadjusted bivariate OLS. In the levels model, income is measured as log growth since 1960, so the constant is the fitted life expectancy at the country’s 1960 income. In the difference model, the constant is the expected five-year gain in life expectancy at zero income growth. Results. In levels, the median slope was 10.0 years per unit of log income (6.9 years per doubling), positive and significant in 92 of 106 countries, with a median R² of 0.81. In five-year differences, the median slope fell to 1.5 and was significant in only 12 countries; the median R² was 0.08. The constant was positive and significant in 72 countries. Pooling all 1,272 country-periods, life expectancy rose 1.34 years per five years at zero growth, and 10% faster income growth over five years was associated with an additional 0.17 years (p = 3.7×10−5; R² = 0.026). Conclusion. Income growth is associated with gains in life expectancy, but the association is small: about a tenth of the average five-year gain. Most of the gain is in the constant, which in this model is the improvement that occurs without income growth.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Across countries, life expectancy rises with income, steeply at low incomes and more slowly at high incomes (Preston, 1975). The previous post in this series showed that within most countries the two series are also strongly correlated over time, with a median within-country correlation of 0.87. It also showed that correlations of year-over-year changes are close to zero, and that a number of countries gained more than 20 years of life expectancy while their GDP per capita fell.

Correlation has two limits for the question that matters for policy. First, it measures the tightness of an association, not its size: a correlation of 0.97 could correspond to a large or a trivial effect of income. Second, in levels it cannot separate an association between income and health from the fact that both tend to rise over time. OLS regression addresses the first limit directly by estimating a slope. Fitting the regression in differences partly addresses the second, because differencing removes anything constant about a country, and the constant term in a difference regression captures the average improvement that occurs regardless of growth.

Objectives

  1. For each country, to estimate the association between the level of GDP per capita and the level of life expectancy, with the constant anchored at the country’s 1960 income.
  2. For each country, to estimate the association between five-year growth in GDP per capita and the five-year change in life expectancy.
  3. To estimate the average five-year association across all countries (pooled), and how much of the average gain in life expectancy it accounts for.
  4. To compare the levels and difference estimates and describe what each can and cannot show.

Methods

Study design

Observational, country-specific time-series regressions, with a pooled regression across countries for the difference model. Each country is analyzed separately, so countries are compared only with their own past.

Setting

Annual data, 1960 to 2024, from the World Bank World Development Indicators (WDI), last updated 13 July 2026. The difference model uses the five-yearly observations 1960, 1965, …, 2020, giving twelve non-overlapping five-year intervals per country.

Participants (units of analysis)

All World Bank economies, excluding regional and income-group aggregates, with both life expectancy and GDP per capita reported for 1960, which is required to anchor the constant. 106 economies met this criterion, and all 106 also had complete data for every five-year point from 1960 to 2020. For the levels model, 105 countries contributed all 65 years; Syria’s GDP series ends in 2022 (63 years). The requirement of 1960 data excludes most countries that became independent after 1960, including the former Soviet republics and much of Eastern Europe, as well as countries without early national accounts (see Bias).

Countries are grouped by the World Bank region listed in the WDI country metadata, used as published.

Variables

Outcome. Life expectancy at birth (LE), in years.

Exposure, levels model. Growth in GDP per capita since 1960, in log points:

\[ x_{ct} = \ln \text{GDP}_{ct} - \ln \text{GDP}_{c,1960}, \]

where GDP is GDP per capita in constant 2015 US dollars for country c in year t. By construction x = 0 in 1960, x = 0.69 when income has doubled, and x is negative when income is below its 1960 level.

Exposure, difference model. Five-year growth in GDP per capita, Δ5x, defined below.

Bias

Five sources of bias are noted. (1) Selection: requiring 1960 data restricts the sample to 106 countries and excludes most post-1960 states; the excluded countries may have different income and health trajectories. (2) Measurement: for many low- and middle-income countries, WDI life expectancy is modeled by the UN Population Division rather than measured annually, which smooths the series and weakens estimated associations with short-run income changes. (3) Confounding: the regressions are unadjusted; education, conflict, disease environment, governance and health spending may affect both income and life expectancy. (4) Common trends: in the levels model, calendar time is an omitted variable (addressed by the difference model). (5) Reverse causation: healthier populations may be more productive, so part of any association may run from health to income.

Study size

All eligible countries were included (106). The levels model uses up to 65 annual observations per country (6,888 country-years in total); the difference model uses 12 five-year changes per country (1,272 country-periods). No power calculation was performed. With 12 observations per country, the country-specific difference regressions have little power to detect small slopes.

Statistical methods

Levels model. For each country c, fit by OLS over its years t:

\[ LE_{ct} = \alpha_c + \beta_c\, x_{ct} + \varepsilon_{ct}. \]

The OLS estimates are

\[ \hat{\beta}_c = \frac{\sum_t (x_{ct}-\bar{x}_c)(LE_{ct}-\overline{LE}_c)}{\sum_t (x_{ct}-\bar{x}_c)^2} = r_c\,\frac{s_{LE,c}}{s_{x,c}}, \qquad \hat{\alpha}_c = \overline{LE}_c - \hat{\beta}_c\,\bar{x}_c , \]

where rc is the within-country correlation from the previous post and s denotes a standard deviation. The slope is the correlation rescaled into units: years of life expectancy per log point of income. In a regression with one explanatory variable, R² = r².

Why the constant is life expectancy at 1960 income. Because x = 0 when income equals its 1960 value, the fitted value at that income is

\[ \widehat{LE}_c\big|_{\,\text{GDP}=\text{GDP}_{c,1960}} = \hat{\alpha}_c + \hat{\beta}_c \cdot 0 = \hat{\alpha}_c . \]

So α is the life expectancy the fitted line predicts at the country’s 1960 income. It is not the same as observed 1960 life expectancy, because the line need not pass through the 1960 point; the two are close when the line fits well.

Anchoring at 1960 changes only the constant, not the slope. If the same regression is written with uncentered log income, LE = a + b ln GDP, then b = β and

\[ a_c = \alpha_c - \beta_c \ln \text{GDP}_{c,1960}, \]

which is the predicted life expectancy at a GDP per capita of one dollar, a value with no meaning. For the United States, a = −10.3 years, while α = 69.3 years, close to the observed 1960 value of 69.8.

Interpreting the slope. A one-unit change in x is a change of one log point, an increase of about 172%. Two more useful scales are a doubling of income (β × ln 2 ≈ 0.69β) and a 10% increase (β × ln 1.1 ≈ 0.095β).

Five-year difference model. Define changes over non-overlapping five-year intervals, t = 1965, 1970, …, 2020:

\[ \Delta_5 LE_{ct} = LE_{ct} - LE_{c,t-5}, \qquad \Delta_5 x_{ct} = x_{ct} - x_{c,t-5} = \ln \text{GDP}_{ct} - \ln \text{GDP}_{c,t-5}. \]

Two things happen when the levels model is differenced. First, the 1960 anchor cancels: (ln GDPt − ln GDP1960) − (ln GDPt−5 − ln GDP1960) = ln GDPt − ln GDPt−5, so the choice of base year has no effect on this model. Second, the constant α cancels too. Suppose the levels relationship also contains a time trend λ, representing improvements in health that accrue each year regardless of income:

\[ LE_{ct} = \alpha_c + \beta_c\, x_{ct} + \lambda_c\, t + \varepsilon_{ct}. \]

Differencing over five years gives

\[ \Delta_5 LE_{ct} = 5\lambda_c + \beta_c\,\Delta_5 x_{ct} + \Delta_5 \varepsilon_{ct}. \]

This motivates the estimated difference model

\[ \Delta_5 LE_{ct} = \gamma_c + \delta_c\,\Delta_5 x_{ct} + u_{ct}, \]

where the slope δ estimates the association with income net of a linear trend, and the new constant γ (= 5λ) is the expected five-year change in life expectancy when income does not grow. In other words, the constant in the difference model measures the health improvement that occurs without income growth, the quantity the previous post could only describe.

Pooled difference model. To summarize across countries, the difference model was also fit to all 1,272 country-periods together, with a single γ and δ. Because the twelve observations from one country are not independent, standard errors were clustered by country:

\[ \widehat{V}(\hat{\theta}) = (X'X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_{g=1}^{G} X_g'\hat{u}_g\hat{u}_g'X_g\Big)(X'X)^{-1}\cdot\frac{G}{G-1}\cdot\frac{N-1}{N-k}, \]

with G = 106 countries, N country-periods and k = 2 parameters; p-values use a t distribution with G − 1 degrees of freedom.

Inference in the country-specific models. Country-specific standard errors are classical OLS standard errors. In the levels model they are too small, because consecutive annual observations are strongly autocorrelated (see the previous post); p-values for β should be read as descriptive. Non-overlapping five-year differences are much less affected.

Sensitivity analyses. The pooled difference model was re-estimated excluding the 2015–2020 interval, because 2020 life expectancy was lowered in many countries by the COVID-19 pandemic; separately for 1960–2000 and 2000–2020; and by World Bank income group (2024 classification).

Results

Participants

106 countries were included: 31 in Sub-Saharan Africa, 26 in Latin America & the Caribbean, 21 in Europe & Central Asia, 13 in East Asia & Pacific, 9 in the Middle East & North Africa, 3 in South Asia and 3 in North America. By the World Bank’s current classification, 39 are high income, 28 upper middle, 25 lower middle and 14 low income.

Levels. The levels regressions look like strong evidence that income raises life expectancy. The median slope is 10.0 years per log point, equivalent to 6.9 years for each doubling of GDP per capita, positive and significant in 92 of 106 countries, with a median R² of 0.81. The constant behaves as the base-year algebra predicts: the median α is 56.7 years, and across countries α correlates 0.89 with observed 1960 life expectancy, typically within about 3 years. Where α departs from observed 1960 life expectancy, it flags an unusual base year. China is the clearest case: observed life expectancy in 1960 was 33.4 years, at the height of the famine of 1959–1961, while α is 56.6, the value its later trajectory implies for 1960 income.

Nine countries have significantly negative levels slopes, the same countries the previous post identified with negative correlations, including Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Haiti, Niger and Liberia. A negative β does not mean income harms health; it means income fell while life expectancy rose.

Five-year differences. Once the trend is moved into the constant, the slope shrinks and becomes imprecise. The median δ is 1.53, about 15% of the median levels slope, and is significant in only 12 of 106 countries (11 positive, 1 negative). The median R² falls from 0.81 to 0.08. In contrast, the constant γ is positive in 105 of 106 countries (median 1.19 years per five years, interquartile range 0.89 to 1.78) and significantly positive in 72; none is significantly negative. For most countries, the regression attributes the bulk of the five-year gain in life expectancy to the constant rather than to income growth.

Across all country-periods, life expectancy rose by 1.34 years per five years when income did not grow (γ), and each additional log point of five-year income growth was associated with 1.74 more years (δ, p = 3.7×10−5). In practical terms, five years of income growth that is 10% faster is associated with 0.17 more years of life expectancy. The association is statistically clear but small: income growth explains 2.6% of the variation in five-year changes in life expectancy. At the sample’s average five-year growth of 0.084 log points, growth accounts for about 0.15 years (1.74 × 0.084) of the average five-year gain of 1.48 years, roughly one tenth.

The results are stable across sensitivity analyses. Excluding the 2015–2020 interval raises γ slightly to 1.44 and leaves δ at 1.53. The constant is smaller after 2000 (1.10) than before (1.45), as gains slow at higher life expectancy, while the income slope is larger after 2000 (2.79 versus 1.38). By income group, the constant is largest in low-income countries (1.66) and smallest in high-income countries (0.96); the slope is largest in low-income countries (3.03) and not significant in upper-middle-income countries.

Discussion

Key results

Three findings stand out. First, the levels regressions reproduce, in units, the strong within-country correlations of the previous post: a median of 6.9 more years of life expectancy per doubling of income. Second, those slopes are mostly trend. When the models are written in five-year differences, which move the common trend into the constant, the median slope falls by about 85%, R² falls from 0.81 to 0.08, and the slope is significant in only 12 countries. Third, the constant in the difference model, the expected gain at zero income growth, is positive and large: about 1.3 years every five years on average, roughly nine tenths of the average gain.

How does the change in GDP affect the change in life expectancy?

On average, positively but modestly. Pooled across 106 countries, a country whose income grew 10% faster over five years gained about 0.17 more years of life expectancy. For the typical country, sustained growth of 1.8% per year, close to the sample median, corresponds to about 0.15 years of the roughly 1.5 years gained every five years. The rest of the gain appears in the constant.

This bears directly on the question raised in the previous post: can health improve without income growth? In this model, the constant is that improvement, and it is the larger part of the story. It is consistent with the literature arguing that most of the twentieth-century mortality decline came from the spread of knowledge, technology and public health programs rather than from income as such (Preston, 1975; Caldwell, 1986; Cutler, Deaton and Lleras-Muney, 2006). It is also consistent with the countries in the previous post that gained 20 years of life expectancy while their incomes fell.

Limitations

These are unadjusted associations and should not be read as causal effects. Several limitations apply:

  • The constant is not policy. γ captures everything that raises life expectancy at a steady pace unrelated to five-year income growth: medical technology, public health programs, education, aid and falling fertility. It identifies room for non-income explanations but does not identify which ones.
  • Income may act slowly. Differencing over five years captures short- and medium-run associations. If income works through slow channels, such as schooling or health systems built over decades, a five-year model will understate its long-run association, and the levels slope may be closer to the truth than it appears. The two models bracket the answer rather than settle it.
  • Attenuation. Measurement error in GDP and modeled life expectancy biases slopes toward zero, and differencing amplifies measurement error relative to the signal.
  • Inference. Levels standard errors are understated because of autocorrelation. Country-specific difference regressions have 12 observations and little power, so the 94 non-significant δ estimates are consistent with small positive effects, not evidence of no effect.
  • Sample and timing. Requiring 1960 data excludes many countries; the final interval includes the onset of COVID-19; and single extreme intervals, such as China’s famine recovery, can dominate a country’s regression.

Interpretation

The levels regression answers “how much higher is life expectancy when income is higher?” within a country, and its answer, about seven years per doubling, mixes the effect of income with everything else that improved over the same decades. The difference regression asks “when income grows faster than usual, does life expectancy also rise faster than usual?” Its answer is yes, but by a small amount. Read together, the two models suggest that income growth has contributed to the rise in life expectancy since 1960, but that most of the rise would be attributed to the steady improvement captured by the constant.

Generalizability

The estimates describe 106 countries with data from 1960, a group that includes most of Africa, Latin America and Western Europe but few post-1960 states. They describe 1960 to 2020, a period of unusually rapid diffusion of medical technology; a similar regression for an earlier or later period could give a different constant. Pooled estimates are averages across very different countries, and Figure 2 shows that country-specific relationships vary widely.

Conclusion and next steps

A simple OLS regression turns the correlation from the previous post into a size: in levels, about seven years of life expectancy per doubling of income; in five-year differences, about 0.17 years per 10% of faster growth, with most of the gain in the constant. Anchoring the levels model at 1960 income makes its constant interpretable as fitted life expectancy at the starting income; differencing removes that anchor and turns the constant into the gain that occurs without growth. Natural next steps are to add country and year effects to a pooled model, which separates global improvements in each period from country-specific growth; to use longer differences (ten or twenty years) to test whether income acts slowly; and to add measured health inputs, such as vaccination coverage or health spending, to see what the constant contains.
