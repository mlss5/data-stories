Wages vs. Prices: A Century of American Pay

Average factory pay and consumer prices, 1900–2025

Data Story  ·  United States  ·  Wages and Prices

An hour of factory work paid 15.2 cents in 1900. The same hour pays $29.51 today — nearly 200 times as much. But prices rose too, almost 39-fold, so the dollar figures alone don’t say much about whether that hour buys more than it used to. Stripped of inflation, the answer is yes, but unevenly: most of the gain happened before 1973, and the half-century since has been close to flat.

Picking the right yardsticks

Before plotting anything, two questions needed answers: whose wages, and whose prices?

For wages, the cleanest candidate would be something like median household income — except the Census Bureau didn’t start publishing that until 1947. Nominal GDP per capita reaches back further, but it blends labor income with capital income and divides by total population rather than hours worked, so it answers “how rich is the country,” not “how far does an hour of work go.” The series economic historians actually use for this comparison is average hourly earnings of production workers in manufacturing. The Bureau of Labor Statistics has published it continuously since 1939; the NBER’s Macrohistory Database and the economic-history site MeasuringWorth extend it back to 1900 by linking two earlier academic series. It’s the standard choice for a century-long view, for the simple reason that nothing else covers the period as consistently.

That choice comes with a real caveat. It’s a manufacturing, blue-collar measure in an economy that has moved overwhelmingly into services, and it counts cash wages only — leaving out the employer-paid health insurance and retirement contributions that have grown from negligible to a large share of total compensation since the 1960s. Total compensation almost certainly grew faster than the cash-wage line below, especially in recent decades; this chart is deliberately the more conservative, more consistently measured slice of the story.

For prices, the Consumer Price Index is the more straightforward call: it’s the standard cost-of-living yardstick, built to track what a household actually spends on housing, food, transportation, and the like. The Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis publishes a continuous CPI series back to 1800, splicing the official BLS index (1913–present) onto three earlier historical estimates the BLS itself compiled for its Handbook of Labor Statistics.

Three eras of pay

The 126-year run breaks into three distinct phases, and the breakpoints aren’t subtle.

1900–1929. Factory wages roughly quadrupled in nominal terms, but World War I inflation took a large bite out of the gain. Real purchasing power still rose by close to 80% over these three decades — a genuine, if uneven, improvement.

The Depression and the war, 1930–1945. Nominal wages fell sharply in the early 1930s, but prices fell even faster, so real wages held up better than the headlines from the period suggest, before climbing again under wartime labor demand and the wage and price controls of the early 1940s.

The postwar golden age, 1946–1973. This is where the real wage line is steepest. Driven by strong productivity growth, high union density in manufacturing, and a tight postwar labor market, real factory pay nearly tripled again between the end of the war and 1973 — the single best sustained stretch in the dataset.

Since 1973. The real wage index has barely moved: 504 in 1973, 502 in 2025. Nominal wages and consumer prices have both continued climbing — nominal pay is up more than sevenfold since 1973 alone — but almost none of that nominal gain has translated into added purchasing power for this particular measure of pay. Economists point to several plausible, overlapping explanations for the slowdown: the shrinking share of the workforce employed in manufacturing itself, declining private-sector union membership, the erosion of the real value of the minimum wage, a shift in total compensation toward non-wage benefits that this wage-only measure doesn’t capture, and the effects of automation and global trade competition on demand for manufacturing labor specifically. There’s no single, settled answer in the research, and the relative weight of each factor remains actively debated.

What this measure can’t tell you

A national average hides as much as it reveals. It says nothing about how pay has diverged by region, industry, education, race, or gender over the same period, and it says nothing about the well-documented widening gap between average and median pay since the 1980s — a separate and important part of the inequality story that a single average wage line cannot show. It is also, deliberately, a manufacturing-sector measure in an economy where manufacturing’s employment share has fallen for decades; a similar exercise using service-sector or all-private-sector pay (available only from 1964 onward) would likely tell a somewhat different recent story. And because it tracks cash wages only, it understates how total compensation — wages plus benefits — has grown, particularly since employer-sponsored health insurance became widespread.
