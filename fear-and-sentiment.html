Fear Gauges and Headlines

Testing a correlation two ways: across time, and across countries

Machine Learning for Social Science  ·  Boston, MA

Two series can move together for a good reason, or for no reason at all — ice cream sales and crime rates both rise in summer, but neither causes the other; a warmer season drives both. This post works through two correlations that hold up better, testing each two different ways: once over time within a single place, and once across places at a single point in time.

Example 1: Market fear and the news, over time

The variables. The CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) is the options market’s 30-day forward-looking measure of expected S&P 500 volatility — Wall Street’s “fear gauge.” The San Francisco Fed Daily News Sentiment Index is a daily sentiment score built by scoring economics-related articles across 16 major U.S. newspapers, smoothed into a continuous time series back to 1980. Both are daily, both are United States–based, and both are free to download.

Sourcing. VIX: CBOE Volatility Index, retrieved from FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis), series VIXCLS. News sentiment: Shapiro, Sudhof & Wilson (2020), Daily News Sentiment Index, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco. The overlap between the two series runs from January 3, 2000 to September 11, 2026 — 6,747 trading days.

The levels of VIX and news sentiment are strongly negatively correlated (r = −0.61): when the market is fearful, the news is gloomier, and vice versa. That is the correlation most people mean when they say “the market and the news move together.” But when the same two series are converted to day-over-day changes — did VIX and sentiment move together from one day to the next — the correlation collapses to essentially zero. That gap is itself the lesson: a strong correlation in levels can come almost entirely from both series drifting through the same multi-year regimes (calm 2000s, the 2008 crisis, COVID, etc.), not from any tight day-to-day linkage between newsroom tone and options pricing.

Example 2: Policy uncertainty and market volatility, across countries

The variables. Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU), the Baker-Bloom-Davis index built from the frequency of policy-uncertainty language in national newspapers, one score per country per month. Paired against each country’s equity volatility for the same month — the VIX-style implied-volatility reading where a clean monthly figure was obtainable.

Sourcing. EPU: policyuncertainty.com, “All Country Data.” Volatility: VIX monthly average from FRED (VIXCLS); German DAX implied volatility from Eurex’s official VSTOXX Derivatives: July 2026 Recap; India VIX from NSE-sourced daily market reports (HDFC Sky), averaged across the month’s available sessions; S&P/ASX 200 VIX monthly average from ASX’s own Group Monthly Activity Report, July 2026. The month is July 2026.1

With only four countries, this correlation carries almost no statistical weight on its own (n = 4 leaves just two degrees of freedom) — it is included to show the mechanics of a cross-sectional correlation, not to make a claim about the world. Worth noticing anyway: Germany has by far the highest EPU reading (roughly three times the next-highest country) but a middling volatility reading, which alone is enough to flatten the linear (Pearson) relationship even though the rank (Spearman) relationship still points positive.

Why the correlation might not mean what it looks like

In the time-series example, the headline result (r = −0.61 in levels, r ≈ 0 in changes) points to a specific third factor: the macroeconomic regime. VIX and news sentiment both drift with the business cycle — both were low and calm for most of the mid-2000s, both moved sharply in 2008 and again in 2020 — so a big share of their correlation in levels is really both series independently tracking the same underlying economic conditions, rather than one driving the other day to day. The fact that the correlation nearly disappears in first differences is the tell.

In the cross-country example, the plausible third factor is different: EPU counts newspaper mentions of policy uncertainty, while an implied-volatility index prices option-market risk. A country can generate a great deal of uncertainty-flavored news coverage — coalition politics, fiscal debates, regulatory fights — without that translating into elevated pricing of equity risk, and Germany in July 2026 looks like exactly that case. The two variables are related in principle (both are trying to measure “uncertainty”) but are constructed from different underlying processes (text mentions vs. option prices), which is enough to decouple them country by country.
